# Business Vision — The CaRCade

A physical multiplayer gaming platform where teams control intelligent RC vehicles inside a large, instrumented arena. The experience is designed to feel less like operating an RC car and more like stepping inside a multiplayer video game.

---

## Core Concept

Teams of people control RC vehicles in an arena that is itself part of the game — interactive elements, lighting, sound, and physical objectives respond to what players do. Participants do not need to be experienced RC drivers, gamers, or particularly athletic. The system is built so people with very different abilities can each contribute meaningfully to a team, through role-based play:

- Drivers
- Navigators (picture blindfolds)
- Strategists
- Communicators
- Coordinators
- Objective specialists
- Team leaders

Roles can change from game to game. The goal is not to identify the best RC driver — it's to create an environment where a diverse group of people can have fun together while naturally exercising communication, strategy, problem-solving, leadership, and adaptation. This inclusivity is a foundational design principle, not a side benefit: the experience should never depend on athletic ability or prior RC experience.

## The Problem This Solves

Traditional RC vehicles are difficult for newcomers — within the first 30 seconds, an unfamiliar driver can go from excited to frustrated and ready to give up. That observation is the origin of the entire concept. The underlying technology exists to remove that specific frustration, not to sanitize the physical experience:

> "The system isn't saying, 'You aren't allowed to crash.' It is saying, 'Go ahead and play. I'll quietly keep small mistakes from taking you out of the game.'"

Crashing, bumping, and spinning out are part of the fun and should remain part of the fun. What the system should prevent is the kind of collision that removes a player from the game, even for a minute.

## Core Technology

- **The puck** — an onboard computer giving each vehicle awareness of its position, movement, and surroundings, and a connection to the arena's game state.
- **Driver assistance** — softens player input only as much as necessary to keep a vehicle participating, without taking control away from a skilled driver or making the vehicle impossible to crash. The technology should be invisible to the player — they simply pick up a controller and drive.
- **Familiar controllers** — a standard PlayStation or Xbox-style controller, integrated into the game engine, so the only new thing being learned is the arena itself, not the input device.
- **Fleet intelligence** — the same onboard sensors that enable driver assistance can flag high-impact crashes, abnormal vibration, and degrading motor/battery performance for predictive maintenance, with backup vehicles able to swap in mid-event so a mechanical issue doesn't stop play.

## Game Design Philosophy

Games are meant to be immersive, instinctive, and self-explanatory — the arena itself should teach the objective through lighting, sound, physical targets, and vehicle behavior, rather than a rules briefing. This matters especially for corporate groups with wildly varying gaming experience.

Racing is only one possible game the underlying platform supports. Other modes under consideration: soccer, team territory capture, objective missions, search & rescue, relay challenges, and a tactical/tank-battle mode with distinct roles (combat, supply, medic/tow, commander, navigator). The platform is meant to support a growing library of games on the same physical infrastructure — this variety is also the core answer to "why would a company come back," rather than relying on the track itself being the draw.

Vehicle personalization (plain bodies + decals, branded/decorated by teams pre-game) is intended both as a fun activity and as a way for participants to physically handle real hardware before controlling it in the arena.

## Target Market & Positioning

Primary target is the corporate events / team-experience market. The pitch is not "convince companies to buy something new" — it's "offer a better answer to a problem companies already spend money solving." Common compromises in existing corporate activities (exciting-but-intimidating, accessible-but-forgettable, team-oriented-but-obviously-artificial, etc.) are the gap this platform is designed to close by combining physical entertainment, competition, teamwork, technology, accessibility, customization, and turnkey service in one offering.

Stated success bar: **"Would employees choose to do this if their employer wasn't paying for it?"**

**Turnkey philosophy:** the target operating promise is "tell me your headcount, date, budget, and preferences — I'll handle the rest," including venue coordination, setup, staffing, food & beverage, and teardown, with tiered service levels (a lean core event vs. full-service add-ons).

## Business Model Elements

- **Two-person core operation** is an explicit target for the base event, with additional staff justified only for larger events or premium service tiers.
- **Off-site vs. mobile** are treated as two deliberate, complementary offerings — off-site as the strongest immersive experience, mobile for organizational reach (multiple offices, remote/distributed teams) — not simply sequential phases.
- **Modularity as a business advantage**, not just an engineering property: the same platform is meant to scale from small mobile deployments to large permanent installations without being rebuilt.

## Launch Strategy

1. **Phase 1 — Mobile/pop-up platform.** The proving ground: test the concept across many environments before taking on the cost of a dedicated facility.
2. **Phase 2 — Venue partnerships** (mad tRaCk was raised as one example of this model) — occupying existing large spaces during otherwise-unused periods rather than building new.
3. **Phase 3 — Dedicated purpose-built venue.** At this stage the platform is no longer really "an RC attraction" — it becomes a physical multiplayer gaming venue in its own right, potentially hosting corporate events, public sessions, robotics programs, and school competitions side by side.

## Pricing — Status: Hypothesis, Not Established

An early customer conversation suggested a turnkey experience in the **$100–150 per person** range could be easy for an HR buyer to evaluate, with more elaborate experiences potentially supporting higher price points. This is explicitly a hypothesis to validate, not a set price. The core pricing principle regardless of the eventual number: **one price, one experience, minimal add-on complexity.**

## Proof-of-Concept Approach

Deliberately slow and methodical, not a rush to a full build:

1. Build and test the driver-assistance/safety system first, in simulation — understand the mechanics before any hardware exists.
2. If the simulation results are promising even in simple form, build a small physical arena (~$1,000 budget) with a prototyped full-tech puck, to confirm the simulated approach blends correctly with real hardware.
3. Only after both of those hold up, shift primary effort toward making the assist system effective *and* close to imperceptible to the player.

## What Still Needs to Be Proven

The original vision document was explicit that market data proves demand exists for in-person corporate experiences — it does not prove this specific business will work. Open questions, treated as hypotheses rather than settled facts:

- Do people genuinely love the experience — both beginners and skilled players?
- Do the games produce real teamwork, not just proximity?
- Do corporate buyers see enough value to pay for it, and does the eventual price actually work?
- Can the operation be delivered efficiently, and can the vehicles withstand sustained commercial use?
- Do customers come back?

The stated discipline: don't mistake an exciting technology demonstration for a validated business. Technology, experience, and business all have to separately hold up.
